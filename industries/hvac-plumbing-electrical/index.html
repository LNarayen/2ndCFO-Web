Serve your customers. Understand what the work costs.

A busy service schedule can hide differences in spending and work mix. 2ndCFO brings bank and card records into a view you can use to review costs and ask business questions. Comparing service, repair, installation, or maintenance margins needs complete revenue, labor, and materials records; technician productivity and dispatch-system integrations are not assumed product features.

Discuss your business

Reliable records, then business understanding

The product starts with bank and credit card CSV imports, retains source rows, and supports confirmed categorization rules and manual corrections. Analysis uses the records available for the selected period. Consistent categories make spending easier to investigate, but they do not make incomplete data complete. Confirm additional service work and its delivery schedule before relying on a report. The purpose is to reduce repeated digging and give you a clearer starting point for decisions while you focus on customers and operations.

Compare work that has different economics

An installation can bring a large invoice alongside significant equipment and labor costs. A repair visit may have a smaller invoice but different travel, diagnostic, and callback demands. Maintenance agreements add another timing pattern. Agree on service categories and cost definitions before comparing their contribution, and check that field records and accounting entries describe the same activity. Potential review topics below depend on the available data and a separately confirmed service scope.

  • Separate service, installation, and maintenance revenue where records allow.
  • Review direct labor, materials, and subcontracted work against the related sales.
  • Distinguish deposits and agreement payments from the timing of work performed.
  • Monitor outstanding customer invoices alongside payments to equipment suppliers.

Look beyond technician revenue alone

Revenue per technician can be useful, but job mix, crew size, overtime, travel, and callbacks affect the interpretation. A high-sales period is not necessarily a high-margin period. Operational comparisons should use consistent definitions and reliable time or job records. Review exceptions with the team before treating a financial ratio as an individual performance verdict.

Plan for seasonality and capacity changes

Peak demand can increase payroll and materials spending before customer cash arrives. A quieter season can leave fixed vehicle, insurance, rent, and staffing costs unchanged. A cash forecast should reflect your own collection history, expected work, supplier terms, and recurring obligations. When considering another vehicle or crew, test the ramp-up period and a lower-demand case alongside the expected results.

Define a practical starting point

Tell us which trades you serve, your mix of repair and installation work, your accounting and field-service systems, and how job costs reach the books. The first priority may be monthly reconciliations, a cleaner service-line breakdown, or planning for a slow season. System compatibility, reporting frequency, and available operating data need to be confirmed in the engagement scope.

Illustrative example

Illustrative example only, not a customer result: If installation revenue increases while cash tightens, compare equipment deposits, supplier payment dates, labor spending, and customer balances due. A profitable installation can still require working capital before final payment arrives.

Common questions

Can you compare service and installation margins?

Discuss how revenue, labor, and materials are recorded today. A reliable comparison depends on assigning those items consistently to the relevant work.

What if we also handle large construction projects?

Project work may need additional reporting for estimates, changes, progress billing, and retainage. The specialty-contractor approach can complement the service-business view.
