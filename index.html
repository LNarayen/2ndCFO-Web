About 2ndCFO · A founder’s story

Why I built 2ndCFO.

Are your books always behind? Do you wonder whether your transactions are categorized correctly?

20+ years operating businesses 5 bootstrapped businesses

Do the numbers leave you confused—and the accounting terminology make things harder? Are you waiting too long to hear back from your bookkeeper?

Do you have to check several banks and financial accounts just to understand your cash flow? Does keeping the records up to date cost more time and money than you think it should?

You’re not alone. I went through this while running my own businesses.

I started as a software engineer, then spent more than two decades bootstrapping five businesses. I had payroll, vendors, customers, and decisions that couldn’t wait. I assumed my financial systems would help. Too often, I was running the business without a clear picture of how it was doing.

01

The answers arrived after the decisions.

Sometimes I wouldn’t know how the business had performed until months after the year was over. The books eventually got done, but I had already made the hiring, spending, and investment decisions they should have helped me make.

Even with reports in hand, answers took digging and spreadsheets—or skilled financial analysis that cost time and money. Owners end up rationing questions: investigate the big problems, leave the smaller ones unexplored. The cost of getting an answer changes which questions a business owner can afford to ask.

I didn’t want to become an accountant. I wanted to become a better business operator.
02

Then I questioned the numbers themselves.

I remember looking at the financials for one of my restaurant businesses and stopping at the food-cost number. It didn’t match the business I knew.

The books reported
~40%
My calculations suggested
~25%

I started digging into how purchases had been categorized. A Costco transaction could mean ingredients, fuel, cleaning supplies, or equipment. The merchant name alone couldn’t tell the whole story.

Instead of the books helping me understand my business, I was investigating the books. A category could make a transaction look finished without explaining what actually happened.

That became a foundational lesson: trustworthy data has to come before intelligence. A sophisticated dashboard can’t make unreliable numbers useful.

03

We were asking at the wrong time.

At tax time, I would receive transactions to review that were already months old. What was this purchase? Why did we make it? Was this the right category? Multiply those questions across a year, and reviewing the books became an exercise in reconstructing memory.

The moment to understand a purchase, attach the receipt, and preserve the explanation was when it happened.

If a transaction needs context, ask while the answer is still fresh.

That is what I want 2ndCFO to do: surface uncertainty early and keep the answer and documentation with the transaction, before the owner has to reconstruct history.

04

An audit took me back seven years.

During a state audit, I was asked for transactions and supporting receipts from seven years earlier. Many of those receipts were in boxes. I spent about a month finding records and matching the evidence to the transactions.

When errors surface during tax preparation, a filing, or an audit, correcting them costs more than professional fees. The owner loses time researching old transactions and finding documentation, while the business absorbs the disruption and the owner carries the stress.

When the books are wrong, the business owner ultimately pays the price. That experience made preserving the receipt, context, and history feel like part of running the business—not just recordkeeping.

05

The engineer in me kept asking why.

Why did so much electronic financial activity still require people to move information, repeat questions, and piece together the same story? Reconciliation and review serve a purpose. I wanted to preserve those controls while rethinking the manual work around them.

I also began asking who needed access to the books. Revenue, payroll, bank activity, and margins reveal the financial life of a company. Handling that information should be deliberate.

Privacy and confidentiality belong in the design from the beginning. For me, that means limiting unnecessary access and considering who needs information, and why, as part of building the system.

My two careers were converging. I had lived the problem as an operator, and my engineering background made me question why it still worked this way.

06

The books should serve the business.

That is why I started 2ndCFO. With organized, trustworthy records already in place, ordinary questions—How are we doing? What changed?—can take far less time and professional work to answer. The value is getting the answer while there’s still time to make a better decision.

Taxes matter too. Those same records should give my accountant organized, well-supported information to prepare them properly.

One trustworthy set of books should serve both the owner running the business and the accountant preparing the taxes.

Business owners shouldn’t have to spend their lives trying to understand their accounting.

Their accounting should help them understand their business.

That’s why I built 2ndCFO.
